UUV · underwater camera calibration

Why the focal length gets multiplied by 1.33

Nothing changes inside the camera. Water changes the direction every ray arrives from — and a pinhole model has exactly one parameter that can absorb it.

a note on the ×1.33 in our calibrationnumbers from calibration/optique.pyD435i in the BR-100230-151 tube

A camera underwater still forms its image the same way. What changed is outside it: at the window, every ray coming from the water is bent away from the normal before it ever reaches the lens.

A pinhole model has one knob that can account for that bend — the focal length. Through a flat window, the value that knob has to take is exactly the refractive index of water, n = 1.33.

01Where 1.33 comes from

Four lines of Snell's law, and no optics beyond it.

  1. 01

    A ray leaves the target and reaches the window at an angle θw from the normal, travelling through water.

  2. 02

    It crosses into the housing. Snell's law across the water–acrylic–air stack gives the angle θa inside. The acrylic in the middle cancels out: it shifts the ray sideways but does not change the exit angle. Only the water index survives.

  3. 03

    The pinhole model puts the image at radius r = f · tan θa. For small angles, where tan ≈ sin, substitute step 02 into it.

  4. 04

    What comes out is a camera in air whose focal length is n times longer. That is the whole factor.

sin θa = n · sin θw    ← Snell, water → air
r = f · tan θa ≈ f · n · tan θw    ← small angles
fwater = n · fair = 1.33 · fair

02What it changes, on our camera

Numbers for the D435i, from the bare-camera calibration in optique.py.

Two consequences follow from the same factor, and both are checkable. The field of view narrows, because a longer focal length sees less. And everything looks 1.33× bigger — the effect any diver notices, where objects seem about a quarter closer than they are.

03The factor is only paraxial

It is exact on the optical axis and drifts steadily away from it.

Step 03 of the derivation swapped a sine for a tangent. That is free at the centre of the image and expensive at the edge: a flat window is not a single-viewpoint system, so no single focal length can be right everywhere. The model stays inside one pixel only out to 8° off-axis (optique.angle_modele_fiable()). Past that, what saves the measurement is the distortion coefficients, which soak up the residual — and they only do so if they were fitted in water. This is the part Treibitz et al. formalise: see the references.

04Where it stops being true — our tube is not a flat window

This is the part worth knowing before anyone applies 1.33 to our calibration.

Everything above assumes a flat port. Our camera does not look through one: the D435i is 90 mm wide and only fits lying down inside a 49.5 mm bore, so it looks out through the cylindrical wall of the tube. A cylinder does not behave the same way in its two directions.

The mistake this page exists to prevent

Applying ×1.33 to both axes would set the vertical focal length 28 % too high. Every distance read from a tag would inherit that error on one axis and not the other, and nothing in the pipeline would complain.

Underwater, this camera is anamorphic by 1.286 — the two axes of the image simply do not magnify by the same amount. No single number describes it, which is why optique.py returns a pair, and why focale_eau() deliberately hands back the smaller of the two.

05So what do we actually do

We do not rely on the factor at all.

The 1.33 is how you understand the problem, not how you solve it. We calibrate the camera in its final state — in the tube, immersed — with python calibration/calibration.py --montage tube_eau, and every script then reads that calibration through MONTAGE_ACTIF. The factor is kept for one job only: as a sanity check that the measured fx came back roughly 1.33× the in-air value. If it did not, something moved.

Which is also why the camera must not slip inside its cradle after calibration: 1 mm of slip is about 1 % on every distance (optique.sensibilite_glissement()).
